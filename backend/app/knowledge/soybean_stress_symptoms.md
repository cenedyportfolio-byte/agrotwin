# Soybean stress symptoms seen from above

## Yellowing patterns
Interveinal yellowing of the upper leaves in patches, often on high-pH or
calcareous soils early in the season, is typical of iron deficiency chlorosis;
plants usually recover as roots develop. Yellowing that starts at the leaf
margins of older leaves suggests potassium deficiency, common on sandy or
low-potassium areas. A pale, uniformly light-green crop with small plants can
mean poor nodulation (wet, cold or compacted soil, or a first-year field
without inoculant) rather than a lack of nitrogen fertiliser. A drone map
shows the pattern and extent of yellowing; a soil and tissue test tells the
cause.

## Water stress
Drought shows first on knolls and sandy areas as a dull, grey-green canopy,
leaves that flip to show their pale undersides, and wilting in the afternoon.
Ponding and poor drainage show in low areas as thin, yellow or dead patches,
frequently with root rot. Both produce low cover in the analysis; the
topography and the location of the zone tell them apart.

## Diseases with a recognisable pattern
Sudden death syndrome appears from R3 onward as interveinal yellowing turning
brown while the veins stay green, often in compacted or wet, cool parts of the
field, with rotted taproots. White mould kills plants in dense canopies after
cool, wet weather during flowering; wilted plants appear in patches and
carry white fungal growth on the stem. Phytophthora root and stem rot kills
plants in wet, poorly drained spots at any stage. Soybean cyst nematode gives
stunted, yellow, oval patches elongated along the tillage direction, mostly on
lighter soils; the cysts are visible on roots as tiny white or yellow lemon
shapes. Drone imagery reveals the patches; identification needs a look at the
plants, and often a laboratory test.

## Insect and physical damage
Defoliation by bean leaf beetle, Japanese beetle or caterpillars reduces cover
without changing colour; hail and wind strip or shred leaves in streaks that
follow the storm direction; deer feeding hollows out field edges. In all of
these the canopy thins, so the analysis flags the area; what happened is
obvious on the ground within minutes.

## Herbicide injury
Injury from drift or carryover shows as uniform stunting, cupped or strapped
leaves, or bleached tissue, usually in stripes or along a field edge next to
the source. Recovery depends on the product and growth stage; note the
pattern, the date and neighbouring applications.
