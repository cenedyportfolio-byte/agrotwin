# Weeds in soybean

## Why early detection matters
Soybean yield loss from weeds happens mostly early: weeds that emerge with the
crop and compete during the first four to six weeks after emergence do the
most damage, and control is far more effective while weeds are small (under
about 10 cm or 4 inches). A drone flight around V2 to V4, before the canopy
closes, is the best time to map weed patches from the air, because vegetation
between the crop rows is still visible and is not crop. Late-season escapes are
visible again once weeds grow above the soybean canopy, and mapping them helps
plan harvest-time seed-bank control and next year's strategy.

## Common broadleaf weeds
Waterhemp and Palmer amaranth (pigweeds) are the most damaging weeds in many
soybean regions: fast growing (Palmer amaranth can add several centimetres a
day), prolific seeders, and widely resistant to glyphosate and other herbicide
groups. Palmer amaranth often has petioles longer than the leaf blade and
smooth stems; waterhemp has narrower, glossy leaves. Giant ragweed emerges
early with large, lobed leaves and grows very tall. Horseweed (marestail)
starts as a rosette, bolts into a tall single stem, and is frequently
glyphosate resistant. Velvetleaf has large, heart-shaped, velvety leaves;
common lambsquarters has mealy grey-green leaves; morningglory vines climb the
crop and interfere with harvest; cocklebur has broad rough leaves and burred
seed heads.

## Common grass weeds
Foxtails (giant, green, yellow), barnyardgrass, fall panicum and volunteer
corn are the usual grasses. They are hard to tell from a distance and from
crop rows in RGB imagery; a scout on the ground identifies them quickly by
leaf, ligule and seed head.

## Using a drone map for weeds
A drone can flag where vegetation grows between rows or above the canopy, and
how large those patches are, but it cannot reliably identify the species from
the image. Treat flagged patches as places to walk to: identify the weed,
estimate its size and density, and record it. Management decisions belong with
the label, the growth stage of crop and weed, local extension guidance and
resistance history; rotating herbicide modes of action and combining
cultural controls (narrow rows, competitive varieties, cover crops, timely
planting) are widely recommended general practices.
